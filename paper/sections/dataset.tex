\section{Data Acquisition}

\label{sec:dataset}



\subsection{Acquisition and composition}



The \textsc{CrackID} frozen benchmark snapshot consists of 140 photographs of 17 exterior walls which were taken at a single location over four half-days using two consumer handheld devices (the data can be obtained through Mendeley Data~\cite{ferramenta2026crackid}). The walls have both stippled render (textured) and smooth painted plaster; in the case of the smooth plaster, the only visible features are cracks. The dataset is split into six validation walls (walls 01 to 06; 66 photos, 66 identities) and eleven test walls (walls 07 to 17; 74 photos, 146 identities). Only those identities that appear in at least two photographs are considered to be answerable queries (18 in the validation set and 19 in the test set; $n_Q{=}305$ in the test set and $439$ in the validation set). Additionally, two further walls (walls 18 and 19) have been included and are therefore omitted from all the figures in this paper. Each wall is recorded by a continuous walk-around, with a median time interval of 3 seconds between consecutive frames and a maximum duration of 81 seconds; it is for this reason that the data includes within-visit viewpoint and scale correspondence, which is regarded as an upper bound on multi-visit performance.



\smallskip

Adjacent-frame retrieval. In the case of the 305 test queries which did have an answer, we use the adjacent frame as the starting point for the retrieval process. As the gaps shown in Section~\ref{sec:results} (ranging from 305 down to 205, then to 149 and finally to 105 for the gaps numbered 0 to 3) make clear, Rank-1 is still achieved as the number of items decreases. This result shows that the method is robust in the face of short-baseline variations rather than indicating a lack of relevance.



\subsection{Annotation, and why it needs auditing}

\label{sec:annotation}



The ORB homography method operates one-to-one, so for each crack, a single click passes from one frame to the next, and the fragment then combines the segments that constitute the individual physical crack. The greatest risk of an incorrect merge is at this stage, such as changing a hard negative into a free positive. Although the number of merges is provided, their accuracy is not. More precisely, 95 of the 212 identities (45%; 70 out of 146, 48% in the test set) result from merges, with median bridged gaps of 228 pixels in the test set and 286 pixels in the validation set.
