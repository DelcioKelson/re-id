\section{Introduction}

Ticket management systems arrange reports of physical defects into records so that they can be inspected, prioritized, interventions carried out and followed up on. Although each ticket should contain the history of a single incident, different users may take photographs of the same crack more than once, which results in duplicate tickets that disrupt the history and lead to redundant assessment work. Whenever a new observation is made it must either be linked to the existing ticket or a new ticket had to be created for damage that has not been recorded.



This is about the visual re-identification (re-ID) of photographic evidence in that (i) the same shape can be recognised even though there are variations in the angle of view, distance, field of view, illumination, or partial visibility and (ii) damage which has changed (by extending, widening or branching) must still be identified, together with the need to tell the difference between damage that has grown and new damage that has appeared. Mistakes in this process lead to duplicates or misattribution.



The existing methods for detecting and segmenting cracks are concentrated on dealing with individual images. Since datasets like CrackSeg9k~\cite{kulkarni2022crackseg9k} and OmniCrack30k~\cite{benz2024omnicrack30k} do not include cross-observation identities, it is not possible to associate observations with tickets. It has not yet been tested whether techniques from person/object re-ID~\cite{zhou2019osnet,qian2024reidtrends} can be applied to cracks—given that different defects may look similar to one another and that one defect can vary considerably.



We have developed \textsc{CrackID} in order to find out if photographs show the same wall crack: 140 photos from 17 walls that were obtained during one visit (6 of these were used for validation and 11 for testing; 19 test identities are available for matching; $n_{Q}{=}305$). Real photos are used to account for the changes that take place during a single visit, while the features relating to appearance and growth are analysed only with the help of synthetic images. The study of cross-visit performance using real photos will be undertaken in future research. All the accuracy figures are point estimates derived from a small sample grouped by wall.



The evaluation looks at ten zero-shot matchers on \textsc{CrackID} by dividing up the candidate retrieval phase from the step where a decision is made about whether or not the defects are the same and by incorporating open-set identification with rejection, since a high rank by itself is not enough to generate a report; it also makes use of the context along with crack-erased control cases to tell the cracks apart from wall-location cues, and a geometric baseline is applied in order to align the walls and then compare the projected masks.



The study looks at Research Question (RQ1), which is about association in the case of acquisition change; RQ2 is concerned with context and geometric alignment; and RQ3 deals with synthetic appearance or growth change. For RQ1 and RQ2 we use real single-visit photos, whereas RQ3 is an exploratory study based on synthetic data. Future work will focus on ticket-level duplicate reduction and cross-visit evaluation.
