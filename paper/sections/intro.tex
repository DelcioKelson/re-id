\section{Introduction}

Building ticket management systems organise reports of physical defects into records that support inspection, prioritisation, intervention, and follow-up. A ticket should maintain the history of one incident, but the same crack may be photographed repeatedly by different users or inspections, spawning duplicate tickets that fragment history and duplicate assessment work.

Addressing this requires associating each new observation with its existing ticket, or creating a new ticket for unrecorded damage.

Photographic evidence makes this a visual re-identification problem. Two cases matter: (i)~unchanged morphology under varying viewpoint, distance, field of view, illumination and partial visibility; and (ii)~physically evolved damage (extension, widening, branching) whose identity must persist despite condition change, distinguishing damage change from acquisition change. Either error creates a duplicate or misattributes the observation.

Existing crack methods address detection and segmentation within single images. Datasets such as CrackSeg9k~\cite{kulkarni2022crackseg9k} and OmniCrack30k~\cite{benz2024omnicrack30k} lack cross-observation identities, so detection cannot link an observation to a ticket. Whether person/object re-identification~\cite{zhou2019osnet,qian2024reidtrends} transfers to cracks---where distinct defects look alike and one defect varies widely---is untested.

We introduce \textsc{CrackID} for deciding whether observations show the same wall crack: 140 photographs of 17 walls in a frozen snapshot (6 validation, 11 test; $19$ matchable test identities, $n_Q{=}280$). Real photos cover acquisition variation within one visit; a synthetic protocol preliminarily probes appearance change and growth. Real cross-visit performance remains future work. All accuracy figures are point estimates on a small wall-clustered sample.

Our benchmark tests ten matchers zero-shot on \textsc{CrackID}, separating candidate retrieval from thresholded same-crack decisions and open-set identification with rejection: ranking a record highly does not justify attaching a report to it. Context and crack-erased controls separate crack cues from wall location, and a geometric baseline registers walls and compares projected masks.

The study asks RQ1 (association under acquisition change), RQ2 (roles of context and geometric alignment), and RQ3 (behaviour under synthetic appearance/growth change). RQ1--RQ2 use real single-visit photos; RQ3 is exploratory synthetic. Ticket-level duplicate reduction and cross-visit evaluation are left for future work.
