\section{Introduction}

Ticket management systems record reports of physical defects so that they can be inspected, prioritized, repaired, and followed up. Ideally, each ticket holds the history of a single incident. In practice, different users may photograph the same crack more than once, producing duplicate tickets that fragment this history and cause redundant assessment work. Every new observation must therefore either be linked to an existing ticket or open a new ticket for damage that has not yet been recorded.

This is a visual re-identification (re-ID) problem with two difficulties. First, the same crack must be recognized despite changes in viewpoint, distance, field of view, illumination, and partial visibility. Second, a crack may itself change---by extending, widening, or branching---and must still be matched to its earlier observations, while genuinely new damage must be distinguished from growth of known damage. Errors in either direction lead to duplicate tickets or misattributed history.

Existing work on crack detection and segmentation focuses on single images. Datasets such as CrackSeg9k~\cite{kulkarni2022crackseg9k} and OmniCrack30k~\cite{benz2024omnicrack30k} contain no cross-observation identities, so observations cannot be associated with tickets. Whether techniques from person and object re-ID~\cite{zhou2019osnet,qian2024reidtrends} transfer to cracks is untested, since different defects may look alike while a single defect can vary considerably in appearance.

To study this, we introduce \textsc{CrackID}: 140 photographs of 17 walls captured during a single visit (6 walls for validation and 11 for testing, with 19 test identities available for matching and $n_{Q}{=}305$ queries). Real photographs capture the acquisition changes that occur within a visit, whereas appearance and growth changes are probed only with synthetic images. Cross-visit evaluation on real photographs and ticket-level duplicate reduction are left for future work. All accuracy figures are point estimates from a small sample grouped by wall.

We assess ten zero-shot matchers using \textsc{CrackID}. The protocol divides the process into two parts: the retrieval of candidate images and the decision as to whether two observations depict the same defect, and it includes open-set identification with rejection since a high rank by itself is not enough to associate a report with a ticket. To test whether matchers recognize the crack itself or only wall location, we use crack-erased control cases together with a geometric baseline which aligns the wall images and compares the projected crack masks.

Three research questions guide the study:

\begin{description}

  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Can the evaluated methods associate observations of the same defect under varying image acquisition conditions?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How do wall context and geometric alignment contribute to defect re-identification?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] How do two selected approaches perform under synthetic changes in appearance and crack growth?

\end{description}

RQ1 and RQ2 are answered with real single-visit photographs; RQ3 is an exploratory study on synthetic data.

The contributions are as follows: (i)~the cross-observation identities of \textsc{CrackID}, together with their annotation provenance (Section~\ref{sec:annotation}); (ii)~a benchmark covering retrieval, same-defect decisions, and open-set identification, which addresses RQ1 (Sections~\ref{sec:protocol} and~\ref{sec:ceiling}); (iii)~context controls, a geometric reference, and a coverage analysis, which address RQ2 (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:controls}, and~\ref{sec:coverage}); and (iv)~a synthetic revisit probe, which addresses RQ3 (Section~\ref{sec:hybridbench}).
