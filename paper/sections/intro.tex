\section{Introduction}







When ticket management systems are built, reports of physical defects are arranged into records which can be used for inspection, prioritization, intervention, and follow-up. Each ticket should contain the history of a single incident; yet the same crack may be photographed more than once by different users during various inspections, leading to the creation of duplicate tickets that disrupt the history and result in duplicate assessment work.







To address this, each new observation must be linked to the existing ticket, or a new ticket must be created for any damage not yet recorded.







This is a visual re-identification problem for photographic evidence. There are two cases to consider: (i) the morphology remaining unchanged even when the viewpoint, distance, field of view, illumination, or partial visibility varies; and (ii) physical damage that has changed (by extending, widening, or branching) yet whose identity must still be retained in the face of such changes, to distinguish between damage that has changed and damage that has been acquired. An error in either case results in a duplicate or misattribution of the observation.







Current methods for detecting cracks focus on detection and segmentation within individual images. Since datasets like CrackSeg9k~\cite{kulkarni2022crackseg9k} and OmniCrack30k~\cite{benz2024omnicrack30k} do not include cross-observation identities, it is not possible to associate an observation with a ticket. The applicability of person/object re-identification~\cite{zhou2019osnet,qian2024reidtrends} to cracks—given that different defects may look similar and one defect can vary greatly—has not been tested.







We developed \textsc{CrackID} to determine whether photographs show the same wall crack: it includes 140 photos of 17 walls taken at one time (6 for validation and 11 for testing; 19 test identities can be matched, with $n_Q{=}305$). Real photos account for variations in how images were acquired during a single visit; we initially use a synthetic approach to investigate changes in appearance and growth. Improving performance across visits using real photos will have to be left to future work. The accuracy figures are point estimates based on a small sample of clustered walls.







The benchmark evaluates ten different matchers in a zero-shot setup on \textsc{CrackID}, separating the process of retrieving candidates from the thresholding step involved in deciding whether cracks are the same, and makes use of open-set identification with rejection: merely ranking a record highly does not mean that a report should be attached to it. By combining context with crack-erased control samples, it is possible to distinguish signals that indicate cracks from those that indicate wall position, and a geometric baseline is used to detect walls and to compare the projected masks.







The study examines RQ1 (the association under acquisition change), RQ2 (the roles of context and geometric alignment), and RQ3 (behavior under synthetic appearance/growth change); for RQ1 and RQ2, real single-visit photos are used, while RQ3 is an exploratory synthetic study. We will address duplicate reduction at the ticket level and cross-visit evaluation in future work.
