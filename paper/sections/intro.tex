\section{Introduction}

Building ticket management systems organise reports of physical defects into records that support inspection, prioritisation, intervention, and follow-up. A ticket should maintain the history of a particular incident, bringing together its observations and the actions taken to address it. However, the same damage may be reported repeatedly by different users or during successive inspections. For example, several photographs of a crack in the same wall may lead to separate tickets, even though they refer to a single physical defect. This redundancy can fragment the incident history, duplicate assessment work, and make it difficult to determine whether an intervention remains pending or whether the damage has progressed.

Therefore, addressing this problem requires associating each newly reported observation with the appropriate existing ticket, while allowing a new ticket to be created when the observation concerns a previously unrecorded defect.

Photographic evidence makes this association a visual re-identification problem: the system must determine whether two observations represent the same physical damage. Two distinct scenarios are common in building inspections. In the first scenario, the damage retains essentially the same physical morphology as in previous observations, but the photographs differ in viewpoint, camera distance, field of view, illumination, resolution, and partial visibility, which can substantially alter the recorded appearance of an unchanged crack. In the second scenario, the damage already has a registered ticket but has physically evolved since the previous observation. A crack may extend, widen, branch, or develop additional connected segments, changing its morphology, geometry and photometrics. Here, re-identification must preserve the identity of the incident while recognising that its condition has changed. This scenario requires distinguishing changes in the damage itself from changes caused by photographic acquisition. In both scenarios, an incorrect association may either create a duplicate ticket for the progressing defect or attach the observation to a different incident.

Existing crack-analysis methods primarily address detection and segmentation: they identify the presence of cracks and delineate their extent within individual images. Datasets such as CrackSeg9k~\cite{kulkarni2022crackseg9k} and OmniCrack30k~\cite{benz2024omnicrack30k} support these tasks but do not provide cross-observation identity labels. Consequently, successful detection does not establish whether an observed crack corresponds to one already documented in a ticket. Appearance-based matching approaches, including those developed for person and object re-identification~\cite{zhou2019osnet,qian2024reidtrends}, offer candidate methods for this association. Their applicability to cracks nevertheless requires evaluation, particularly when different defects have similar local appearances or when the same defect is observed under changing acquisition conditions or physical progression.

We introduce \textsc{CrackID}, a dataset for evaluating whether different photographic observations correspond to the same wall crack, supporting the visual association required by building ticket management. The real dataset addresses changes in acquisition conditions within a single inspection visit. A complementary synthetic protocol provides a preliminary assessment of appearance variation and damage progression; performance across real successive inspections remains to be established.

Our benchmark compares ten matching methods without training them on \textsc{CrackID}. Evaluation distinguishes retrieving the correct candidate from deciding whether two observations represent the same crack, and includes cases in which no corresponding record exists. This distinction is essential for ticket management: placing an existing record near the top of a candidate list is insufficient to justify attaching a new report to it. Experiments with surrounding wall context and crack-erased images further examine whether matching depends on the crack itself or on its location. A geometric reference baseline makes location explicit by registering photographs using wall texture and comparing projected crack masks. The results motivate the use of spatial context alongside local appearance, while registration failures and shared annotation provenance limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

The study is guided by three research questions. RQ1: Can the evaluated methods associate observations of the same defect under varying image acquisition conditions? RQ2: How do wall context and geometric alignment contribute to defect re-identification? RQ3: How do two selected approaches perform under synthetic changes in appearance and crack growth? The first two questions are investigated using real photographs acquired within a single inspection visit. The third provides an exploratory assessment under controlled synthetic transformations. Together, these questions examine the visual association component needed to link new reports to existing defect tickets, while leaving performance across real successive inspections and the reduction of duplicate tickets for future evaluation.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions:} (i)~the \textsc{CrackID} crack re-identification dataset with cross-observation identity labels and an explicit account of annotation provenance (Sec.~\ref{sec:annotation}); (ii)~a benchmark evaluation of ten matching methods that separates candidate retrieval, same-defect decisions, and identification when no corresponding record exists (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}, \ref{sec:ceiling}); (iii)~context controls and a geometric reference baseline for examining the roles of crack appearance, wall location, and registration coverage (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:controls}, \ref{sec:coverage}); (iv)~a preliminary synthetic revisit evaluation that explores appearance variation and damage progression beyond the real dataset's within-visit scope (Sec.~\ref{sec:hybridbench}).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~II reviews related work. Section~III describes the \textsc{CrackID} dataset acquisition and annotation, and Section~IV defines the benchmark protocol and evaluation metrics. Section~V presents the geometric reference baseline. Section~VI reports the crop-baseline benchmark and the wall-context controls (the appearance ceiling). Section~VII compares geometry against appearance, analyses registration coverage and failures, ablates the geometric design, and reports the synthetic revisit experiments. Section~VIII discusses validity, limitations, and future research, including real cross-visit data and integration into ticket lifecycle evaluation. Section~IX concludes the paper.
