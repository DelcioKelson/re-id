\section{Conclusion}

Maintenance systems that keep a record per physical defect must re-identify that
defect when it is photographed again. For wall cracks there was no dataset on
which to test whether that is possible, so we built one: \textsc{CrackID}, 140
photographs over 17 walls with 212 crack identities, released with its
annotation provenance stated rather than assumed --- $45\%$ of identities were
formed by an automatic merge, and we report that rate rather than presenting the
labels as ground truth by construction.

The benchmark's main finding is negative and, we think, structural. Ten matchers
across five families all decide \emph{same crack or not} at a pairwise $F_1$
between $0.305$ and $0.359$ --- a $0.054$-wide band across two decades of method
development and a $2590\times$ range in compute --- while Rank-1 spans five times
as much and is uncorrelated with it. Ordering a gallery well and deciding an
identity are not the same capability, and only the second maintains a record.

Our geometric reference baseline shows the ceiling is not a property of the
task. A homography from non-crack wall texture, crack masks projected into a
common frame, and scale-free Chamfer agreement under global assignment reach
$\mathrm{DIR}@\mathrm{FAR}{=}0.1$ of $0.768$ against $0.414$, while answering
only $48\%$ of pairs. The advantage has a specific source: an aligned pair
supports a \emph{licensed negative} --- project the recorded crack, look,
observe it is not there --- where a crop embedding can only report low
similarity. We expect that to matter for any defect marking a rigid, textured
surface. Two bounds remain, and both are addressable: a second capture session
weeks later turns \textsc{CrackID} from a viewpoint benchmark into a
cross-visit one, and the annotation audit is instrumented and awaits its
verdicts.
