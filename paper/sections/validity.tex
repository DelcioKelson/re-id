\section{Validity, Limitations, and Future Work}
\label{sec:validity}

This benchmark establishes a first measurable formulation of crack
re-identification; several directions remain open and represent natural
extensions of the present work.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Scaling to multi-wall and multi-visit datasets.}
The test split covers $19$ matchable identities across $11$ walls, which is sufficient
to establish the structural pattern (thresholded decisions near the floor;
open-set benefit of explicit alignment) but not to rank individual methods with
statistical confidence; all accuracy rows are point estimates with no
wall-level intervals. A larger dataset --- more walls, more surface
families, and more annotators --- would allow wall-level confidence intervals
to be reported and method rankings to be interpreted reliably. More
importantly, the current benchmark captures one continuous walk-around per
wall; a second capture session at deliberately varied distances and lighting
would convert the within-visit measurement into a genuine cross-visit
benchmark. That real revisit data is the most valuable next step for
assessing the re-identification component of operational ticket management
backends.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Annotation quality and provenance-free labelling.}
Annotation quality was actively managed: a single human annotator placed
and reviewed all 2,095 clicks from homography-propagated prefill until
zero provisional points remained, and every one of the 1,023 clicks no
component claimed was subsequently triaged against the imagery --- 916
(89.5\%) sit on visible cracks the segmenter missed rather than labelling
mistakes, leaving 107 clicks (10.5\%, 95\% CI [8.7\%, 12.5\%]) naming no
crack. Single-annotator; agreement unmeasured. The reported geometric
advantage must therefore be read as provenance-conditioned until a small,
manually verified subset labeled entirely without
homography propagation quantifies how much is independent of annotation
provenance. Releasing the
per-pair score matrices alongside the dataset would additionally allow
third parties to run sensitivity analyses on how much false-positive mass
would be needed to manufacture an open-set gap of the observed magnitude,
and to compute wall-clustered intervals and threshold-free AP/AUROC.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Closing the registration coverage gap.}
The geometric baseline currently answers $48\%$ of query pairs and scores
zero on smooth-plaster walls. Future work should explore surface-adaptive
registration strategies --- including learned local features, direct
alignment on photometric gradients, or sparse depth from consumer
devices --- that could push coverage toward full-set evaluation.
A learned front-end replacing SIFT would also decouple the geometry
pipeline from the homography-based annotation provenance.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Learning to re-identify from geometry-derived supervision.}
The geometric baseline is a deliberate non-learned reference; it is not a
deployment target. A natural next step is to use the registration pipeline
as a teacher to generate pseudo-labels for training a learned matcher, or
to fine-tune a foundation-model backbone on crack pairs with homography-
derived correspondence. This would let the system inherit the
location-based advantage while remaining applicable to pairs the registration
pipeline cannot answer.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Integration into ticket lifecycle evaluation.}
End-to-end evaluation in a ticket management context requires measuring not
just retrieval accuracy but downstream impact: how often does a
mis-identification open a duplicate ticket, and how often does a false match
suppress a new defect? Future work should define task-level metrics
(duplicate rate, missed-new-defect rate) and evaluate the full pipeline ---
segmentation, re-identification, and ticket assignment --- in a closed-loop
simulation that reflects real inspection workflows.
