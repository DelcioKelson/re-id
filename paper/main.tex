\title{CrackID: A Benchmark for Crack Re-Identification,\\
and the Limits of Appearance Matching}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{C\'atia Loureiro\thanks{Corresponding author.},
Ant\'onio Silva, Beatriz Miranda, and Paulo Brito}
\IEEEauthorblockN{D\'elcio Ferramenta, Nuno da Costa, Ricardo Rodrigues,
and Duarte Fernandes}
\IEEEauthorblockA{DTx Colab, Campus de Azur\'em, Universidade do Minho,
4800-058 Braga, Portugal}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automated maintenance systems turn photographs into tracked defect records: a
crack re-photographed on a later visit must be matched to its existing record
or filed as new. Crack \emph{detection} is mature, but we are aware of no
dataset or benchmark for crack \emph{re-identification}. The obvious
approach, cropping the region and matching embeddings, imports an assumption
from person and vehicle re-ID that may not hold here: that a crack has a
stable, locally distinctive appearance.

We introduce \textsc{CrackID}: 140 photographs of 17 walls covering 212 crack
identities, released with an explicit annotation-quality report and stated
chance levels for every metric. Each wall was captured in one continuous
walk-around, so the benchmark measures \emph{within-visit} viewpoint
robustness. A synthetic multi-visit protocol (warping plus grown or corrupted
appearance) serves only as a secondary check, and cross-session
re-identification remains a hypothesis, not a result, in this paper.

Benchmarking nine matchers across four families, we find that on the decision
a deployed system actually has to make (same crack or not, at a fixed
threshold) none beats chance by more than $0.054$ pairwise $F_1$. Ranking
metrics do separate the methods, but the thresholded decision that matters for
deployment sits near the floor for almost every crop-based method. These are
point estimates over an effective sample of $19$ identities across $11$
walls; wall-level intervals are wide (Sec.~\ref{sec:validity}), so the claim
we make is about the pattern, a cluster at the floor with one exception, not
the individual digits.

Two controls help localize the difficulty. Giving an embedder the surrounding
wall context raises its open-set rate from $0.168$ to $0.404$, but
\emph{erasing the crack itself} from that same view recovers almost all of
the gain ($0.393$): such a method is retrieving on location, not on crack
appearance. A geometric reference baseline, which registers on wall texture
with cracks masked \emph{out}, then Chamfer-matches the projected crack masks
under global assignment, reaches $\mathrm{DIR}@\mathrm{FAR}{=}0.1$ of
$0.768$, against $0.429$ for the best crop method and $0.000$ for a control
that exploits registration coverage alone without examining any crack. The
open-set gap is therefore attributable to matching, not to partial coverage:
coverage-only assigns a constant score to every registered pair and ranks
unregistered pairs last, so its nontrivial Rank-1 reflects registration
overlap rather than crack evidence. As with the $F_1$ results, these are
point estimates on the same $19$-identity sample, and the claim is the $0.35$
gap as a structural finding, not a precise interval (Sec.~\ref{sec:validity}).

This is not deployment evidence yet. The geometric system answers only $48\%$
of pairs, fails outright on two smooth-plaster walls, and its apparent
within-visit advantage is entangled with the homography-based provenance of
our own annotations, a circularity that our two partial checks constrain but
do not remove. On the synthetic revisit check, our only multi-visit evidence,
OSNet$+$context instead wins on both ranking and open-set performance, so the
geometric method's advantage here should not be read as a demonstrated
solution for cross-visit re-identification.
\end{abstract}
