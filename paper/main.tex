\title{CrackID: A Benchmark for Crack Re-Identification,\\
and the Appearance Ceiling It Reveals}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Paper ID \textemdash{} \textit{under review}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automated building-maintenance systems increasingly convert photographs into
tracked records: a defect is photographed, a ticket is opened, and follow-up
activity accumulates against that record. Any defect re-photographed across
visits therefore needs re-identification --- a new photo must match the record
for that specific physical defect, or open a new one. Wall cracks are a common
and difficult case. Yet despite crack detection being mature, we are not aware
of a dataset or benchmark for crack \emph{re-identification}, and the natural
approach --- crop the region and match embeddings --- imports an assumption from
person and vehicle re-ID that may not transfer: that the object has stable,
locally distinctive appearance.
We introduce \textsc{CrackID}: 140 photographs over 17 walls with 212 annotated
crack identities, released with an explicit annotation-quality report rather
than as unaudited automatic labels. Benchmarking ten matchers across classical
keypoints, learned correspondence, general vision embeddings, a re-identification
architecture, and a purpose-built crack-shape descriptor, we find pairwise $F_1$
confined to a $0.054$-wide band ($0.305$--$0.359$) while Rank-1 spans five times
as much and is uncorrelated with it ($\rho = 0.02$). We argue this ceiling is a
property of the data, not of any one method. As a geometric reference baseline
we register on wall texture with cracks masked \emph{out}, then Chamfer-match
projected crack masks under global assignment: it reaches a
detection-and-identification rate of $0.768$ at $10\%$ false alarm against
$0.414$ for the best crop-based method, while answering only $48\%$ of pairs ---
partial coverage we show by censoring to cost accuracy rather than buy it.
\end{abstract}
